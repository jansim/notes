## Summary of Contribution

The paper presents the results from a study of 85 participants' perceptions of algorithmic fairness. The authors employed hypothetical scenarios of ML cancer screening models with varying performance across two subgroups (Race A and B) and had participants rate the subjective fairness of three resulting options of systems with varying performance levels across the two groups (1: same, matching highest; 2: same, average across both groups; 3: different performance). The authors systematically varied the performance scores (accuracy) across the two subgroups and the availability of data (i.e. one group having 3x the amount of data than the other).
They found that participants did not always choose equal scores across models as the most "fair" option and that instead they opted to preserve differences in performance if separately trained models already displayed these performance differences.


## Quality

This work provides an interesting analysis of perceptions of algorithmic fairness and puts into question the general notion of equating fairness with equality between groups. It posits interesting ideas and takeaways, such as the relevance of data-related information e.g. quantity to allow people to form perceptions of fairness. While the authors support their claims with an empirical study, I find some of their arguments lacking in strength and have concerns regarding some of their findings.

**Major points**

One of the central assumptions in this paper is that group-specific performances can serve as an operationalization of distributional differences between groups. In my opinion the paper does not make a sufficiently strong case that this assumption actually holds. In practice, algorithmic performance can depend on a variety of factors and while it is influenced by base rate and availability of data it is not solely a function of these two. We do not know to which degree participants are aware of these differences and whether participants equated group performances with distributional differences beyond the pilot study.

Relatedly, the authors posit (L274) that differences in data availability provide information on the legitimacy of performance disparities. I strongly disagree with this statement, as the legitimacy of group differences is a highly context- and domain-specific issue. How and which data is collected and available is strongly influenced by sociotechnical context and this nuance must be acknowledged when discussing the legitimacy of group differences.

While the paper mentions methods of algorithmic fairness, this discussion could benefit from additional references and a deeper in-depth discussion, positioning the paper's definition of algorithmic fairness relative to other notions. Especially L111 "more advanced and granular definitions" is quite a strong claim and should be expanded to be more concrete. If other metrics are better, should they have maybe been included / discussed in this study? Descriptions of related work often remain at surface level and would benefit from a deeper description / engagement with the literature e.g. L139 "several studies have attempted to identify the most appropriate fairness definition [...]"; which sort of definitions were examined and how?

**Methodological concerns**

As noted in Section 3.5, out of 380 participants data from only 85 is retained. While 186 did not complete the survey, this leaves 194 participants who did, of whom less than half passed the inclusion criteria. As the inclusion criteria seem rather arbitrary e.g. taking 9 minutes to complete the survey, I would recommend the authors to justify their choices, provide more detailed information on how many participants failed each step and, most importantly, demonstrate robustness of the results to different exclusion / inclusion criteria.

The authors use a t-test on data from a 7-point Likert scale. As I understand it they use direct ratings for this test (as opposed to some composite score), so the data is not continuous and therefore not a good fit for a t-test. A non-parametric alternative would produce more convincing results.

Examining the figures, I was surprised to find standard errors across across conditions this uniform. Have the authors checked the validity of the code / data (providing code and data would considerable strengthen the paper) here and investigated the distribution of ratings?

**Minor points**

The different options explored in the paper do not fully reflect realistic trade-offs seen during ML model design and a wider array of options would have benefitted the interpretation of the paper. Perfectly equal performance between models may be considered an unrealistic target. Similarly, Option 1 of matching the highest subgroups' performance is usually not a readily-available option. Option 1 is also wrongly attributed to the performance-fairness trade-off, when this should probably be Option 2 (L306)?

The authors mention the sample based limitations of their work in Section on participant information (which is great), but do not bring this up again in the abstract, introduction or discussion / implications. Mentioning this in at least one of these places would make it easier for readers to position the paper.

It may be worth updating a few of the older references of preprints to their published articles e.g. [13], [22].


- I would very recommend the authors to include analysis code and data with their submission.
- The papers makes strong interpretations out of rather sparse data, 
- The papers posits interesting ideas and takeaways, such as the relevance of data-related information e.g. quantity to allow people to form perceptions of fairness

## Clarity

The paper is generally clearly written and can be understood well.  However, it uses some terms rather loosely, sometimes blurring the line between fairness metrics with metrics of performance. While these are not necessarily unrelated they can and often do correspond to different notions and better disambiguation between terminology would improve the clarity of the paper.

**Major Points**

Similarly base rates, acceptance rates and performance metrics are loosely equated with each other in certain Sections e.g. Section 5, L499 onwards. The nuances between these different kinds of metrics and the metrics within each kind are very important to algorithmic fairness as a field. Talking about "legitimate distribution differences" in this light does not do justice to these metrics when differences in performance versus base rates correspond to *very* different real-world notions.

Figures in the paper are very difficult to read due to exclusively using numeric labels for subplots. Replacing numbers with actual labels e.g. 1 -> NA/NA; 2 -> 90/90; ... for Fig. 1 would significantly increase legibility of figures. Using equal Y-axes, ideally starting  at 0 would also help with representing results more truthfully.

**Minor points**

- The manuscript could benefit from copy-editing to improve its clarity e.g. Section 2 has aprupt jumps between sections and the description of the COMPAS dataset seems out of place  L118 
- L138: "the traditional fairness setting", something like "algorithmic decision making" might be a more suitable label to describe the setting
- L223: survey bias seems to be the wrong term, order effects seems more suitable
- How exactly option 3 is presented for instances with equal subgroup performance is not quite clear (L301). Is there a fixed 10% difference, if so, which particular values are displayed.
- L487 "much of the reasoning referred to [...]" which reasoning? Does this refer to information from the pilot study?
- L386: As far as I know the reproducibility of the Dunning Kruger Effect has been put into question. The authors reasoning here seems reasonable, but if the term is used it should be adequately referenced and the authors should check the latest information on its validity.





Not clearly grounded on the larger body of fairness literature. Their definition of fairness (metric) should be linked back to the literature.
## Originality and Impact

The work provides a novel perspective and idea and empirical data to support it. Unfortunately it lacks a strong connection to and grounding in the fairness literature, so the results are difficult to properly integrate into the body of knowledge. Some of the weakly supported, yet strong assumptions make it difficult to trust its reasoning and the potential methodological issues further threaten the validity of its findings.
